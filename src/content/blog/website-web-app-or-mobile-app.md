People say "app" for almost everything now. "We need an app" can mean a website that brings in customers, a system for staff to manage orders, or something customers download from the Play Store. Those are very different projects, at very different prices.

Choosing the right one first saves money and months. This guide explains the difference in plain terms and gives you the questions that decide it.

## The three, in plain terms

### A website

A website presents your business. People find it on Google, read about what you do, see your work and get in touch. Most visitors read rather than sign in. It can still do useful things, such as contact forms, bookings or taking deposits, but its main job is to inform and convince.

**Good for:** being found online, credibility, and bringing in enquiries. Every business should have one.

### A web app

A web app is something people sign in to and use. Think of a customer portal, a booking system, an online store's back office or a school management system. It runs in the browser on any phone, tablet or computer, so there's nothing to install. When you update it, everyone gets the new version straight away.

**Good for:** tools and systems people use to get something done, especially staff at desks and customers who visit occasionally.

### A mobile app

A mobile app is installed from Google Play or the App Store. It sits on the home screen, sends notifications, and can make full use of the phone: the camera, location in the background, and working offline.

**Good for:** things people use often, sometimes several times a day, and anything that depends on the phone's features or must work without a connection.

## Six questions that decide it

### 1. How often will people use it?

This is the most important question. People install apps they use every day or every week: banking, ordering, chatting. For something they use once a month, or once, asking them to install an app is a hurdle. A website or web app opens from a link.

### 2. Do you need to be found on Google?

If new customers will search for you, you need a website with real pages that search engines can read. An app on its own won't bring in search traffic.

### 3. Who are your users, and where are they?

Office staff, bursars and managers working at a computer are well served by a web app. Riders, field officers, sales agents and drivers who are out all day are better served by a mobile app, especially if they need maps, photos or offline use.

### 4. Do you need the phone's features?

Live location while the app is in the background, scanning with the camera, regular notifications, or working with no signal all point to a mobile app. If you don't need any of these, a web app will usually do the job for less.

### 5. Will people install it?

Many people are careful with data and with storage on their phones. Asking someone to download an app before they've seen any value is a big ask. If you're not sure people will come back often, prove the idea on the web first.

### 6. What's your budget and timeline?

A mobile app costs more than a web app with the same features. It has to work on Android and iOS, be tested on real phones, and pass app store review for every release. If budget is tight, launch on the web and add apps once there's demand.

## Common combinations

Most growing businesses end up with more than one, built in stages:

- **A website first,** to bring in customers, with a **web app** added later for customers to log in, book or track orders.
- **A web app first,** to prove the idea and learn what users need, with **mobile apps** added once people are using it regularly.
- **Mobile apps plus a web platform,** where different users need different things. A delivery business might give customers and riders mobile apps, while staff manage orders, payments and reports in a web dashboard. One backend serves them all.

## Some examples

- **A law firm or consultancy:** a website. It needs to be found, look credible and bring in enquiries.
- **A clinic:** a website with appointment booking, then a web app for patient records and billing.
- **A school:** a web app for staff, with SMS to reach parents. A parent app can come later. See [what to look for in school management software](/blog/school-management-software-ghana).
- **A microfinance or susu business:** a mobile app for field officers who collect in the community, with a web back office for approvals and reporting.
- **A restaurant or shop:** a website or online store, with mobile money at checkout.

## Still not sure?

Start with what your users need to do, not with the technology. If you can describe who will use it, how often and where, the right answer is usually clear.
